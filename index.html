Interactive primer · ~20 minutes

There is no best time series model.
Only the right one for this series.

Most forecasting courses teach you six algorithms. Almost none teach you how to pick one — and picking is the part that actually decides your project. This page teaches the decision procedure, with a live series generator wired into a real (if simplified) recommender.

01

The series decides

Trend, seasonality, its period, noise shape, sample length, horizon, and whether you have external drivers. These are measurable in seconds.

02

The rules are a shortlist

Diagnostics narrow a dozen families down to two or three. They cannot tell you which of the last two wins — the data has to answer that.

03

The backtest is the verdict

Rolling-origin evaluation on your actual data is the only unbiased signal. Everything before it is a hypothesis about where to look.

How to use this page. Everything is live. The panel in Lab generates a synthetic series from knobs; every chart, every number, and the recommendation below are computed from that series in your browser — nothing is hardcoded. Change a knob and watch the advice change. That is the concept.

Step 1 · Build a series

Start with the series, not the model

The single most useful habit: before naming any algorithm, describe the series in structural words. "Level with a rising trend and a stable 12-cycle, whose swings widen as the level rises." That sentence already implies two algorithm choices before you write a line of code.

Step 2 · Structure

Every series is four things stacked

Classical decomposition splits a series into level + trend + seasonality + noise. Nearly every family in the toolbox is a different way of writing those same four terms, which is why they compete with each other.

yt = Lt + Bt + St + et   ·   additive:   Lt · (1 + St) · (1 + et) = multiplicative

Read the four panels for your current series. The question to answer is not "how good is the fit" — it is "which components exist, and at what period?"

Additive vs. multiplicative — how to tell

Look at whether the amplitude of the swings grows with the level. If July is +500 when you're at 1,000 and +5,000 when you're at 10,000, that's multiplicative: use seasonal='mul', or take logs and model additively. If the swing is roughly +500 either way, additive is right.

This is exactly why the AirPassengers series uses trend='add', seasonal='mul': growth is linear, but seasonal amplitude is proportional.

Two seasonalities? Don't average them away.

A daily-energy series has a daily cycle (m=24) and a weekly cycle (m=168). A retail series has weekly and yearly. Plain Holt-Winters assumes one period and will underfit or, worse, blur them.

When two periods are real, the answer is decomposition: strip one, model the other, add back — or fit a Fourier-term model that can hold both at once.

Step 3 · Diagnostics

The six numbers that pick the family

All of these are computed live on the series in the Lab. Learn to read the value, not just the verdict.

Stationarity, informally

A stationary series has a stable mean and stable variance over time — its plot looks like noise around a fixed line. Most classical models assume (or achieve via differencing) stationarity.

The quick visual test: does the centre drift? Do the swings widen? If yes, you need differencing (ARIMA) or explicit trend/seasonal terms (ETS) before residuals mean anything.

ADF:  Δyt = γ + Σ aiyt−i + Σ bjΔyt−j + ε
H₀: γ = 0 (unit root — non-stationary). Reject when t > −2.86.

Interpretation pressure: "non-stationary" is not a verdict against a model. It's an instruction — it means your series needs differencing first, which is precisely what ARIMA's d does.

Diagnostics narrow the field; they do not rank within it. Two families can satisfy every signal equally well. That's not a failure of the diagnostics — it's the definition of a shortlist.

Step 4 · The candidates

Ten families, and the job each one does

Read the "fails when" column as seriously as the "use when" column. Choosing a model by its strengths alone is how you end up debugging Prophet on a random walk.

Where the overlap actually is

This is the part that surprises people. On a clean seasonal series with a few hundred points, ETS, SARIMA and Prophet frequently land within a couple of percent of each other. The choice is then decided by things diagnostics can't see:

  • Interpretability — a level/trend/seasonal breakdown you can explain to an operator beats a black box.
  • Uncertainty — do you need calibrated prediction intervals, or just a point forecast?
  • Maintenance — changepoints and holidays that need handling every quarter?
  • Latency and cost — refitting a big model nightly on 500 series is a different problem from fitting once.
  • Panel scale — one shared model across many related series is a completely different recommendation from one model per series.

Diagnostics tell you the shape of the answer. These constraints tell you which of the finalists you can live with.

Step 5 · The recommender

Diagnostics → ranked shortlist, with reasons

This is the actual rule engine, running on your current series. Each candidate is scored by weighted rules; every score contribution is attached to a human-readable reason. The reasons matter more than the ordering — a recommendation you can't justify is one you'll ignore when it matters.

Scoring is illustrative, not tuned. Real weights come from backtesting across many of your own series.
Step 6 · The verdict

Rolling-origin backtesting

All the rules above produced hypotheses. Now we test them on this series using rolling-origin evaluation: train on the past, predict a fixed window ahead, slide forward, repeat. Every error below is out-of-sample and from a forecast the model could genuinely have made at the time.

The single most common evaluation mistake: holding out one final block and splitting once. That's a single sample of model performance — the difference between two models on one split is mostly luck. Two models that differ by 3% on one holdout are tied.

Choosing a metric without fooling yourself

  • MAE — error in your units. The safest default. Robust to outliers.
  • RMSE — punishes large misses harder. Use when big misses are genuinely worse (capacity, safety).
  • MAPE / sMAPE — dangerous. Blows up when the actual value is near zero, and it's meaningless when the denominator is a price or a level you don't control. Intermittent demand breaks it outright.
  • MASE — scales error by the in-sample naive forecast. Best default when comparing across series with different scales.

Whatever you pick, compare against the naive forecast. A model that can't beat "tomorrow = today" doesn't deserve deployment, no matter how elegant it is.

Horizon changes everything

The best model at h=1 and the best model at h=90 are frequently different families. A short horizon is dominated by recent noise (ETS/AR shine). A long horizon is dominated by trend and seasonality and by whether your exogenous drivers are even knowable that far ahead.

cycles needed for seasonal terms
n / m < 2 → seasonal parameter unidentifiable
n / m < 4 → seasonal parameter unstable

This ratio is the most useful single feasibility check in the whole framework, and it's the one most often skipped.

Step 7 · Traps

Failure modes you will meet in week one

Each of these is a real problem, not a hypothetical. The first four are drawn from the classic lab sequence this guide was written alongside.

Exogenous variables you don't have at forecast time

Using actual future values of a driver during evaluation is oracle leakage. It answers "what if I knew tomorrow's weather perfectly?" — which is not a forecast, it's a measurement. The model will look excellent and then fail in production on the first week you have to guess.

Fix: forecast the driver first, use that forecast as input, and accept the error — or restrict the horizon to where a genuine driver forecast exists (weather is credible at days, not months). If a driver has no forecastable future, it isn't a driver; it's leakage. Also check: drivers must be known for the test period, and correlations with the target that appear only late in the sample are usually leakage rather than signal.

leakage silent
More seasonal cycles than the data supports

Fitting a seasonal model on sub-daily data is the classic version. A 10-minute series with seasonal_periods=144 and 2,000 rows gives you 14 cycles — barely above the identifiability floor, and expensive: a seasonal ARIMA like (1,0,1,144) can take minutes to converge and still be unstable.

Fix: resample first. Aggregate 10-minute to hourly (m=24, plenty of cycles) or to daily, and model at that grain. If sub-daily resolution is genuinely required for the decision, build a hierarchical model: daily base + intra-day profile — not one flat seasonal term.

n/m < 4
Yearly seasonality requested on less than two years of data

You cannot learn an annual cycle from a single year. A tool will happily accept yearly_seasonality=True on 12 months of data, fit something, and report a number — the parameter is unidentified and the "seasonality" it learned is really just the trend absorbed and re-emitted.

Fix: assert it in code — refuse seasonal periods where n/m < 2 before you ever call fit(). This is a one-line guard that prevents a wrong answer delivered confidently.

confident nonsense
Treating a near-random walk as a trend

Prices, exchange rates and many sensor streams have no exploitable structure: the best forecast is today's value. Fitting a rich model to them doesn't add information, it adds variance — and the fitted "trend" is noise that will confidently extrapolate.

Fix: check the differenced ACF. If it's a spike at lag 1 and nothing else, the series is a random walk: forecast with the naive method, report wide intervals, and say so in the writeup. Choosing naive is a finding, not a cop-out.

variance without skill
Seasonal differencing you didn't need

If the seasonal AR terms already model the cycle, adding D=1 in the seasonal block over-differences, inflates the order, and can push the model past the edge of estimation. Seasonally-differencing a stationary-seasonal series is the classic case.

Fix: let an automated order search explore it rather than hardcoding (1,1,1)(1,1,1,m) everywhere — and always check whether the extra differencing improved out-of-sample error, not just AIC.

over-parameterised
MAPE as the headline metric

MAPE divides by the actual value. When actual values can approach zero — intermittent demand, low-volume SKUs, or any series where the denominator is a price — the metric explodes and ranks models by noise. It also asymmetrically punishes over-forecasts.

Fix: MAE or MASE as primary, MAPE as a secondary sanity check on series where the denominator is well away from zero.

misleading
Fitting on the test set by accident

Imputing, normalising, or decomposing the whole series before splitting puts test-set information into the transform. With ffill() across a gap spanning the split boundary, your training labels absorb test-period values.

Fix: fit every transform on training data only — imputation, scaling, decomposition, feature construction — then transform the test set. And fill forward, never backward, across the boundary.

silent

Step 9 · Build it

From this page to the starter kit

This document is a working prototype of the recommender you described: profile → rules → shortlist → backtest, with the "backtest" stage honestly flagged as the only one that settles ties. Porting it to Python is mostly translation, because every concept is already fixed.

The three decisions worth getting right

  • Hard refusals vs. soft warnings. If n/m < 2, refuse the seasonal model outright and say why. A model ranked last but still listed will get used at 2am.
  • Reasons are the API. Return Recommendation(model, score, reasons, config) — not a bare string. The reasons are what make the tool trustworthy and reviewable.
  • Keep the backtest pluggable. The shortlist comes from rules; the verdict comes from your own series. Where those two disagree, the backtest wins — and that disagreement is the most informative thing the tool will ever tell you.
